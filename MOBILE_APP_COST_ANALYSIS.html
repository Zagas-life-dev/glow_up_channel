Build vs. Wrap — In-House + AI

Turning UP into a mobile app

What it costs to put Glowup/UP on iOS and Android with your own team and AI assistance. Grounded in a read of the real codebase, not a generic estimate.

Revised 3 September 2026 · Next.js 16 + React 19 (88,291 LOC) · Express/Mongo API (71,299 LOC) · main @ a39d1c7 · 3 contributors · decision: zero payment in-app, web only

Verdict — Capacitor. Cash cost ~$124–$1,100. Payments decision just removed your biggest risk.

Taking payment out of the app is the right call and it is worth more than it sounds. Apple Guideline 3.1.1 — the In-App Purchase rule and its 15–30% cut — was the one item that could have sunk a submission outright. A free app with no purchase mechanism is the simplest thing either store reviews. It also simplifies your privacy labels and kills the IAP question permanently. Effort drops to ~20–33 days.

Two things worth checking before anyone deletes. Deprecating resource.paymentLink from the website as well as the app ends premium-resource sales outright — the only item on the list that removes revenue rather than dead code. And your keep-list names subscriptions/initialize but not verify, which the Paystack flow needs to complete a transaction; deleting it would break the flow you are keeping. Both in §07.

You are right about the code, wrong about the calendar. The chain static export → native shell → device QA → store review cannot be parallelised away: ~5 weeks of critical path before a reviewer opens your build. One month gets you into TestFlight / Play internal testing; public availability is 5–7 weeks, gated by paperwork rather than code. Start Apple and Google enrollment today — before any code.

Effort is no longer your constraint — sequence is

20–33 days of effort across 2–3 people looks like three weeks. It is not, because the work does not parallelise: phase 2 must finish before 3, 3 before 5, and 5 before 6. Adding a third developer to that chain does not shorten it. Even with unlimited people, the critical path is ~5 weeks — and that is before a single store reviewer opens your build.

Dropping payments took ~3 days off the effort but almost nothing off the critical path, because phase 4 was already running in parallel with phase 3. What it genuinely bought you is risk: fewer review cycles at the far end, which is where weeks actually disappear.

05Why Capacitor, not React Native or Flutter

The recommendation does not change under an in-house AI-accelerated model — it gets stronger, because AI leverage is highest precisely where Capacitor does its work and lowest where a rewrite does.

OptionReuseEffort with AIAssessment
Finish the PWA 100% 1–2 days Week 1 Installable, offline shell, push on Android and iOS 16.4+ once installed. No stores, so no discovery and no store cut — but it is two days from shipping and it de-risks everything after it.
Capacitor ~95% 20–33 days Recommended Your web build inside a native shell with real plugins for push, deep links and browser. One codebase, both stores, desktop later via Electron if wanted.
React Native / Expo ~25% 110–170 days No Only lib/ and api-client.ts survive; all 220 components get rebuilt. AI helps less here than you would hope — rewriting 220 components is 220 design decisions, each needing review, not one repeated transform.
Flutter ~0% 150–230 days No Dart. Even api-client.ts is rewritten, and you would maintain two languages and two copies of business logic that must stay in agreement forever.

Note the asymmetry: AI cuts Capacitor's timeline by ~50% but a rewrite's by only ~35%. Repetitive, well-specified transforms compress hard. Novel design decisions do not — and a rewrite is 220 of those.

07Payments: full deprecation, plus a web-only remainder

Two different operations, and the distinction matters for how the work is done. Deprecated means deleted from the product — website and app both, no flags, no conditional builds. Web-only means the code stays and keeps working on the website, but is excluded from the mobile build.

OperationApplies toMeaning
Deprecate Wallet subsystem · paid promotions · premium-resource paymentLink Deleted from website and app. Straight removal — ~865 LOC across 10 files. No per-platform logic, because the feature ceases to exist anywhere.
Web-only work-with-us (8 files) · founder-batch (2) · subscriptions proxies · components/payment-details.tsx Unchanged on the website; excluded from the mobile build. Costs nothing extra — these routes were already being cut from app scope in §09, so exclusion is the same route list you were writing anyway.
Resolved — Guideline 3.1.1 and the 15–30% cut

With no purchase mechanism in the app, the IAP rule stops applying. This kills the largest single risk in the original plan, permanently removes the 15–30% commission question, drops phase 4 from 5–10 days to 1–2, and simplifies your privacy nutrition labels and Data Safety form (no financial data collected in-app).

The web-only surfaces cost you nothing on mobile — work-with-us, founder-batch, the subscriptions proxies and payment-details.tsx are B2B and marketing pages whose audience is already on a desktop. They were leaving app scope regardless, so "web-only" adds no work beyond the route exclusion list you were writing anyway.

Confirm before deleting — premium resources lose their purchase path entirely

Deprecating resource.paymentLink from both surfaces means premium resources stop being purchasable on the website too, not just on mobile. That is a revenue decision, not a mobile one, and it is the only item on the deprecation list that removes an income stream rather than dead code.

Two ways to land it, and they are materially different: make those resources free (keep the catalogue, drop the gate), or unpublish them (remove the listings). If premium resources earn meaningful money, say so and this one should come back off the list — the mobile requirement is satisfied by excluding the CTA from the app build alone, which needs no change to the website at all.

Resolved by the deprecation — the premium resource link

app/resources/[id]/page.tsx:194 resolves its "Access resource" button as resource.paymentLink || fileUrl. On a premium resource that was an outbound purchase CTA for digital content on a consumer route you are keeping — precisely the shape Apple's anti-steering rules target, with no physical-goods exemption to hide behind. paymentLink is not in your keep-list, so deprecating it removes the last purchase pathway from app scope. Drop the paymentLink branch and let the button resolve to fileUrl only.

One correction — three subscription routes are load-bearing

Your keep-list names app/api/subscriptions/initialize/route.ts but not its three siblings. Do not deprecate verify — a Paystack transaction is not complete until it is verified, so deleting it breaks the initialize flow you are keeping. status and cancel manage the lifecycle of those same subscriptions. All four are thin proxies routed through lib/api-client.ts:372; they are one feature, not four. Keep the set.

Sequence the backend teardown carefully

ProviderWallet and WalletTransaction are Mongo collections holding real historical balances and transaction records. Unmount the routes first, export the collections, then drop the models — in that order, with the export archived. Providers who topped up in the past may have unspent balance, and "the wallet is gone" is a different statement from "the money never existed." Confirm that reconciliation is settled before the models go.

Now your top risk — Guideline 4.2, "Minimum Functionality"

With payments gone, this is the only serious rejection path left. Apple rejects apps that are a website in a shell. The tempting shortcut is Capacitor's server.url pointed at your live site — it ships in days and it is the most likely reason you now get rejected. If the one-month target tempts anyone toward it, that is the moment the month turns into three.

Week 2's real static export, bundled and working offline, plus native push and deep links, is what makes this defensibly an app. That is why week 2 is not skippable. A payment-free app is also a thinner-looking app to a reviewer — so native push and offline behaviour are now carrying more of the "this is a real app" argument than they were.

08Do the feed fix in week one regardless

The feed fans out client-side, then ranks on the device

The feed calls opportunities, events, jobs and resources as four parallel requests from lib/api-client.ts, then lib/ranking/ re-ranks the merged result on the client. On desktop broadband that reads as fine. On Nigerian mobile data — high round-trip latency, packet loss, sleeping radios — per-request latency dominates, and a native app makes it worse: cold starts have no warm browser cache to hide behind.

Ship on the current feed path and the app will feel slower than the website, and reviewers judge apps on first-launch feel. It is 2–4 days with AI, it makes the website faster the same day, and it removes your largest "why does the app feel bad" risk before you have built the app.

10Bottom line

  • Cash: ~$124 to ~$1,100. Two developer accounts and, most likely, a Mac. That is the entire budget.
  • Effort: 20–33 developer-days, roughly half the non-AI figure — the saving concentrated in the static export, the PWA, the feed fix and CI.
  • Calendar: four weeks to TestFlight, five to seven to a public listing. The gap is D-U-N-S paperwork, store review and device QA — none of which respond to AI or to adding people.
  • The payment decision was the single best move in this plan. It removed the 15–30% cut, the IAP build, the dual-rail gating and most of your rejection risk, and it turned phase 4 from ten days of engineering into two days of deletion.
  • Today's action is administrative, not technical: start Apple organisation enrollment, register the Google Play organisation account, and confirm who owns a Mac. Those three gate the month more than any code you write this week.

The thing worth internalising: AI moved your bottleneck. It used to be building 220 components. Now it is a Dun & Bradstreet form and an App Store reviewer — and neither of those gets faster because your team got faster.
