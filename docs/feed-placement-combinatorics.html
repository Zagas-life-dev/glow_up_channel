The anonymous feed is a different, smaller object

Pool size is Σ min(60, eligible_of_type), so it saturates at 240. Past roughly 1,600 listings the feed stops changing in any way: the same 240-item ceiling, the same 3.2 × 1035 page-1 space, the same ~59 slots. Additional inventory buys zero variety — it only lowers the odds that any given listing is ever seen.

Shelf life rewrites the scaling story

The table above assumes inventory is a number you grow. It is not — it is a flow. Listings expire, so live inventory settles at ingest rate × shelf life, and the measured shelf life is short.

TypeMedian days to deadlineClosing ≤14dUndatedAge of live stock

The catalogue replaces itself roughly monthly. That bounds the growth curve: reaching 5,000 live listings needs about 10× today's ingest, not merely time. It also means the governing number is ~10 new listings per day — what it takes to hold 214 steady at a three-week life. If ingest stalls, the feed empties within a month. Ingest rate, not the pool cap, is the first-order control on feed size.

It cuts the other way too. With a three-week life a listing gets one chance: missing the pool is not "buried, will resurface" — it is dead. And resources are the exception that breaks the pattern: undated, never expiring, median age 185 days. They are the only type whose inventory grows monotonically, and therefore the only type where the coverage collapse in the table above is genuinely inevitable.

6What breaks first

Correction

The cap excludes by score today, not by recency

An earlier draft of this report said exclusion was by recency. That is wrong at current scale. .sort({createdAt:-1}).limit(400) only binds once a type passes 400 live listings, and the largest type has 102. Today's 102 → 60 cut for events is made after scoring, by sort((a,b) => b.score - a.score).slice(0, 60).

The recency cliff is real, but it sits at 400 per type rather than here. What bites today is the composition of the score itself.

recommendationService.js:231, :23, :574

Highest impact

Urgency is 10% of the score that decides who makes the pool

Median shelf life is 19–26 days, so exclusion from the pool is terminal rather than deferred. Yet the cut is made on a score where timing carries 10 of ~100 points — interest 30, location 20, career stage 20, skill 15, timing 10, engagement 5. An event closing in two days competes on interest-match against one closing in three months, and can lose.

Fix: replace the flat slice(0, 60) with a deadline-banded quota — N from closing ≤7d, N from 8–30d, N from later or undated — so time-critical content reaches the pool regardless of match score. The bands also scale on their own as volume grows.

recommendationService.js:880-919, :574

Correctness

Expired listings still pass the eligibility filter

Six events with past dates are in the eligible set right now. isValid is written true at creation and nothing flips it back, and neither the opportunity nor the event query carries a date guard — only jobs do. Expired items therefore consume pool slots and reach the orderer, where they land on EXPIRED_WEIGHT instead of being absent.

Fix: give opportunities and events the same $or deadline guard the job query already uses.

recommendationService.js:570, :626 · eventController.js:214

Scaling — deferred

The recency cliff at 400 per type, and the evergreen types that hit it

Past 400 live listings in a type, SCORING_CANDIDATE_CAP starts cutting by createdAt before anything is scored, and exclusion becomes permanent and relevance-blind. For time-bound types this is survivable — newest broadly means live. For resources it is fatal: they never expire, so the collection only grows, and a two-year-old guide is still perfectly good.

Fix: sort the candidate window by actionable date rather than createdAt (index dates.applicationDeadline), and give resources seeded rotation. rotationService.js exists but serves promotions only.

recommendationService.js:23, :574 · rotationService.js:24

Placement bug

Sponsored slots reflow under the reader as pages load

spread = max(4, ⌊n/(p+1)⌋) is recomputed over the entire list on every render, and both n and p move with infinite scroll. The slot at position 4 carries promotion 0 at depth 15, is empty at depth 75, and by depth 95 every one of the 23 slots is empty. Keys shift with the assignment (sponsored-promoted-0 → ad-0), so the cards remount.

A reader who scrolls back up sees different sponsored content than they scrolled past, and the promoter's impression at that position evaporates.

lib/feed-ads.ts:47 · app/home-client.tsx:619

Design vs. behaviour

The rail delivers the burst it was rewritten to prevent

feed-ads.ts documents its spread as giving promoters "presence for the length of the session rather than a burst at the top". The dedup added later in promotedRail removes any promotion already on screen — and the scatter's 0.9 lead bias puts promotions on screen early by design. The two cancel: rail stock falls 10 → 6 → 3 → 0 by depth 95, so all paid rail placement lands in the first two pages.

app/home-client.tsx:499 · lib/feed-ads.ts:12-30

Density

Two promotion systems stack, and page 1 lands at 38% paid

Page 1 renders 18 cards: ~3.8 organic promoted (lead bias + 20% share) plus 3 filled rail slots = 6.8 paid cards, 37.9%. Neither layer is misbehaving on its own — PROMOTED_SHARE is 0.20 and the rail cadence is 1-in-4 — but nothing caps their sum, so the stated 20% intent nearly doubles exactly where it is most visible.

This gets worse as promotions sell: at 40 active promotions the rail stays stocked far deeper, and the top-of-feed share climbs with it.

Holding up

Spacing, completeness and front/back parity are sound

Minimum observed promotion gap was 4 across every run, matching PROMOTED_MIN_GAP; 171 of 172 pool items reached page 1 at least once in 20,000 draws, so nothing is structurally unreachable within a pool; and the frontend constants track scatterRankingService.js exactly, so a server-ordered feed and a client-ordered one behave identically.
